I'm drawn to data science because the same core questions show up everywhere once you start looking for them. Who is likely to stop buying, what's driving a health outcome, which products tend to get purchased together, what separates a bestseller from a book that never finds an audience. Different industries, same underlying work: pulling a real signal out of data that's rarely clean or complete.

That range is intentional. Working as a Junior Engineer at a large retail company gave me an early, practical sense of how much value sits in transaction and customer data that nobody has had time to fully explore. But I didn't want my coursework to only mirror what I see at my job, so I pushed myself toward problems outside retail too: predicting diabetes risk from health survey data, classifying fake news articles by their language patterns, comparing NBA players by position, and studying childcare pricing trends across the country. Each one demanded a slightly different approach, and together they taught me more than staying in one lane would have.

My M.S. in Data Science at Bellevue University gave me the foundation to move between these problems with some confidence. Courses like Statistics for Data Science and Data Exploration and Analysis trained me to interrogate a dataset before trusting it. Data Preparation and Data Mining showed how much unglamorous work happens before a model ever gets built. Predictive Analytics and Applied Data Science tied that groundwork to real forecasting and classification problems, and Data Presentation and Visualization kept reminding me that an insight is only useful if someone outside the analysis can understand it.

The projects below are where all of that came together, from retail-specific work to problems in health, publishing, and sports.
